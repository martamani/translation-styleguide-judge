[S01] My name is Marta. I've been learning pottery on the wheel for two years. The truth is, I'm in no hurry. It takes a long time to master the technique, to understand how the clay behaves. Even to distinguish when a piece is well-made and see how, with practice, the lines become more refined.

[S02] I came to this by chance. One day I was walking and I stumbled upon a workshop across from the beach in my city. I'd seen pottery wheels in movies, and once on a trip I tried one. I don't have a particular vocation for this, just curiosity.

[S03] I'd never done this before, and I'd say it helps you appreciate the beauty of things better. There's also something magical, incredible about it. You see how a lump of clay that's nothing can be transformed into an object that can be useful, beautiful, and evocative.

[S04] At my mother's house, there were some very large ceramic pieces. Jars and pots that came from my grandmother's village in the Galician countryside, in northern Spain. My mother used to tell me that when she was a child, they would borrow oxcarts to transport the pieces from Gundivós, where they were made, and you could hear the clanging of the pieces against each other along the way.

[S05] My pieces are made according to the tradition of Buño, another Galician village that has been dedicated to pottery for many, many centuries. I would like to learn how to make the traditional pieces, which have beautiful shapes, and which evolve over time. Because nowadays, nobody keeps hot chocolate in a chocolate pot, or churns milk in a special container to make butter, or takes ceramic horns to football matches to cheer on their team. Nor do they have shiny brown plates with yellow designs at home, which is typical here. There are more colors. That doesn't mean the ceramic pieces are useless. It simply means that, over time, the essence remains, but the surface changes.

[S06] On the following pages, you can see different pieces I've made. There aren't many, but they are quite distinct from each other.

[S11] Step-by-step instructions

[S12] First, the clay is kneaded, formed into a ball, and placed on the potter's wheel. It is centered, the clay is raised and lowered, and a hole is made in the middle. Then it's opened and shaped by hand, and finally refined with a piece of half a bamboo cane.

[S13] Once finished, it's placed in the kiln for bisque firing, which means a first firing to harden it. Next, it's glazed or coated with porcelain. This makes it usable, prevents water from seeping through, and ensures it's non-toxic. A bisque-fired piece is fragile and breaks easily, so it needs to be fired again to harden it, and then it's ready. Keep in mind that it's not earthenware. Although you can use it every day, it's more fragile.

[S14] The production time is approximately one month. The pieces aren't exactly the same because I don't use molds; I work by hand.
