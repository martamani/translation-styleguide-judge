[S01] My name is Marta. I’ve been learning pottery on the potter’s wheel for two years. To be honest, I’m in no rush. It takes a long time to master the technique and understand how the clay behaves. It even takes time to tell when a piece is well-made and to see how, through practice, the lines become more refined.

[S02] I got into this by chance. One day I was out for a walk and came across a workshop by the beach in my town. I’d seen pottery wheels in films and had tried one out once whilst travelling. I don’t have a particular calling for this; it’s simply curiosity.

[S03] I’d never done this before, and I’d say it helps you appreciate the beauty of things more fully. There’s also something magical, something incredible about it. You see how a lump of clay that’s nothing at all can be transformed into an object that serves a purpose, is beautiful, and evocative.

[S04] At my mother’s house, there were some very large ceramic pieces. Jars and pots that came from my grandmother’s village, in the interior of Galicia, in northern Spain. My mother used to tell me that, as a child, they would borrow ox-drawn carts to transport the pieces from Gundivós, where they were made, and that you could hear the pieces clattering against one another along the way.

[S05] My pieces are made in the tradition of Buño, another village in Galicia that has been dedicated to pottery for many, many centuries. I’d love to learn how to make the traditional pieces, which have such beautiful shapes, and to see them evolve over time. Because these days, nobody keeps hot chocolate in a chocolate pot, or churns milk in a special butter-making container, or takes ceramic horns to football matches to cheer on their team. Nor do people have shiny brown plates with yellow designs in their homes, which is typical of this region. There are more colours now. That doesn’t mean the pottery pieces are no longer useful. It simply means that, over time, the essence remains, but the appearance changes.

[S06] On the following pages you can see various pieces I’ve been making. There aren’t many, but they’re quite different from one another.

[S08] Material: Buño clay, porcelain, Mayco FN-006 blue glaze
Size: 24 cm high x 18 cm wide, 14 cm in diameter
Weight: 1,050 grams
Colour: blue glaze on the outside, white porcelain on the inside.

[S09] Heat-resistant. Suitable for use in the microwave and dishwasher, but must not be placed directly on the hob.

[S10] This teapot is inspired by Fauvist paintings, with their vivid colours and straight lines.

[S11] Step-by-step guide

[S12] First, the clay is kneaded, shaped into a ball and placed on the potter’s wheel. It is centred, the clay is raised and lowered, and a hole is made in the centre. It is then opened up and shaped by hand, and finally refined using a piece of half a bamboo cane.

[S13] Once finished, it is placed in the kiln for bisque firing – the first firing to harden it. It is then glazed or coated with porcelain. This makes it suitable for use, prevents water from seeping through, and ensures it is non-toxic. A bisque-fired piece is fragile and breaks easily, so it must be returned to the kiln to harden it, after which it is ready. Bear in mind that it is not earthenware. Although you can use it every day, it breaks more easily.

[S14] The production time is approximately one month. The pieces are not exactly the same, as I do not work with moulds but by hand.
