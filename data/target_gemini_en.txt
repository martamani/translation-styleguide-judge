
[S01] My name is Marta. I’ve been learning wheel-thrown pottery for two years now. To be honest, I’m in no rush. It takes a long time to master the technique and understand how the clay behaves. Even just learning to tell when a piece is well-made and watching how, with enough practice, the lines become finer and cleaner.
[S02] I stumbled into this by chance. One day I was walking along and found a studio right across from the beach in my city. I had seen pottery wheels in movies and tried one once during a trip. It’s not that I have a special calling for it; I was just curious.
[S03] I had never done anything like this before, and I’d say it helps you appreciate the beauty of things a bit more. There’s also something magical and incredible about it. You watch a completely shapeless lump of clay transform into an object that can actually be useful, beautiful, and evocative.
[S04] My mother’s house used to have very large pottery pieces. Large jars and pots that came from my grandmother’s village, inland in Galicia, in northern Spain. My mother used to tell me that when she was a child, they would borrow oxcarts to bring the pieces from Gundivós, where they were made, and you could hear the clattering of the pieces against each other all along the way.
[S05] My pieces are made following the tradition of Buño, another village in Galicia that has been dedicated to pottery for many, many centuries. I’d love to be able to learn how to make traditional pieces—which have beautiful shapes—and see them evolve with time. Because nowadays, nobody stores hot chocolate in a traditional chocolate pot, or churns milk in a special container to make butter, or brings ceramic horns to soccer games to cheer on their team. People don't really have shiny brown plates with yellow patterns in their homes either, which is the typical style here. There are more colors now. That doesn't mean these pottery pieces are useless. It just means that, over time, the essence remains, but the surface changes.

[S11] Step-by-step process
[S12] First, the clay is wedged, shaped into a ball, and placed on the wheel. You center it, throw it up and down, and make a hole in the middle. Then you open it up, shape it with your hands, and finally refine it using a piece of bamboo half-round molding.
[S13] Once finished, it goes into the kiln for bisquing, which means a first firing to harden it. Next, it is glazed or coated in porcelain. This makes it usable, prevents water from seeping through, and ensures it's non-toxic. A bisque-fired piece is fragile and breaks easily, so it has to go back into the kiln to make it durable, and then it's ready. Keep in mind that this is not earthenware. Even though you can use it every day, it breaks more easily.
[S14] The production time is approximately one month. No two pieces are exactly alike because I work with my hands rather than molds.
