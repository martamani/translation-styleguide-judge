# Styleguide — Cerámica de Marta
## Spanish → English

**Scope:** All web copy for the site: about page, product descriptions, process description, care statements.
**Source language:** Spanish (Spain)
**Target language:** English (international / UK-leaning spelling)
**Version:** 1.0

Every rule has an ID. Use the ID when logging a violation.

---

## 1. Voice and register

The source is written by the maker, in first person, plainly. She undersells. She says she came to pottery *por casualidad*, that she has *no special vocation for this, just curiosity*. This modesty is the voice. It is not a gap to be filled.

**VOICE-01 — Do not inflate.**
Do not add evaluative or promotional language that is absent from the source. Forbidden unless present in Spanish: *passion, passionate, lovingly, carefully crafted, each piece tells a story, timeless, artisanal, curated, journey, soul.*

**VOICE-02 — Preserve hedging and understatement.**
*La verdad es que no tengo prisa* → "The truth is I'm in no hurry," not "I take my time perfecting each piece." Hedges (*diría que*, *simplemente*, *un poco*) are retained.

**VOICE-03 — First person singular throughout the about page.**
Never shift to third person ("Marta creates…") or to a studio "we."

**VOICE-04 — Repunctuate where English grammar requires it. Never to smooth the rhythm.**
Spanish juxtaposition may need breaking where it would produce an ungrammatical English sentence. That is a grammatical necessity. It is not licence to merge, lengthen, or regularise sentences for flow — see PACE-01 and PACE-03. Content may not be summarised, reordered, or omitted.

---

## 2. Forms of address

Spanish source uses *tú* (*podrás ver*, *puedes usarla*, *ten en cuenta*).

**ADDR-01 — Render as direct second person, unmarked.**
English has one *you*. The translation must not compensate for the lost T/V distinction by adding informality markers ("Hey," contractions where the source is neutral) or formality markers ("one may," "the customer should").

**ADDR-02 — Imperatives stay imperative.**
*Ten en cuenta que no es loza* → "Note that this is not earthenware," not "Customers should be aware…"

**TERM-02 — `horno` is context-dependent.**
This is the single most error-prone term in the corpus. Same Spanish word, two English words, decided by who owns the appliance:

- The maker's kiln, in the studio: → **kiln**
  *se mete en el horno para bizcocharla* → "it goes into the **kiln** to be bisque-fired"
- The customer's domestic appliance, in the care statement: → **oven**
  *puede usarse en el horno convencional* → "can be used in a conventional **oven**"

Never "oven" for the studio kiln. Never "kiln" for a customer's oven.

**TERM-03 — Galician forms are retained, not translated.**
*cunca* and *cunquelo* are Galician names for specific traditional vessels. Keep them in italics, unglossed in headings, glossed on first use in body text.

- Heading: *Cunquelo* blanco → "White *cunquelo*"
- Body, first use: "the *cunquelo*, a small handleless cup"

Never "small bowl," "little cup," "cunquelo bowl."

Headings carry the English form only. Do not set the Spanish name alongside it (*not* "Cunquelo blanco / White cunquelo").

**TERM-04 — Place names are not translated or accented away.**
Buño, Gundivós, Galicia. Keep diacritics. Do not gloss ("the village of Buño" is acceptable where the source says *la aldea de Buño*; adding "in northwest Spain" where the source doesn't is a VOICE-01 violation).

**TERM-05 — Throwing: prefer "wheel-thrown."**
*cerámica en torno* → "wheel-thrown" (e.g. "wheel-thrown stoneware"), not "pottery on the wheel" or "pottery on the potter's wheel."

"Wheel-thrown" is the standard term in product copy, artist bios, and gallery labels. It is compact and idiomatic. Use the verbal form ("thrown on the wheel," "throw on the wheel") only where a compound adjective is grammatically awkward — e.g. *Llevo dos años aprendiendo cerámica en torno* → "I've been learning to throw on the wheel for two years."

## 4. Do not translate

**DNT-01** — Brand names: **Mayco**. Capitalised, never lowercased, never translated.
**DNT-02** — Glaze references: **FN-006**, **FN-001**. Format `FN-NNN`, hyphen retained, digits unchanged.
**DNT-03** — Place names (see TERM-04).

## 5. Numbers, measurements and units

**MEAS-01 — Metric is retained. No conversion to imperial.**
24 cm stays 24 cm. Do not add "(9.4 in)".

**MEAS-02 — Restate the unit on every figure.**
Spanish drops it: *30 cm de alto x 20 de ancho*. English must not.
→ "24 cm high × 18 cm wide" — **not** "24 cm high x 18 wide".

**MEAS-03 — Use the multiplication sign ×, not the letter x.**

**MEAS-04 — Space between figure and unit.** `24 cm`, not `24cm`.

**MEAS-05 — Thousands separator.**
Spanish `1.050 gramos` → English **1,050 g**. The decimal point in Spanish is a thousands separator. Rendering this as "1.050 g" is a factual error of three orders of magnitude.

**MEAS-06 — Diameter.** Spanish *10 cm de diámetro* → "10 cm in diameter". Do not use the ⌀ symbol.

**MEAS-07 — Centuries.** *siglo XVI* → "16th century". Roman numeral in the product name (**Jarra blanca XVI** → "White jug XVI") is a proper name and stays; the descriptive sentence converts.

## 6. Fixed strings

**FIX-01 — The care statement is a fixed string.** It appears on every product and must be byte-identical each time:

> Heat resistant. Microwave and dishwasher safe. Do not place on direct heat.

Any variation across products is a violation, even if each variant is individually acceptable English.

**FIX-02 — Field labels are fixed:**
Material · Size · Weight · Colour

Note **Colour**, not *Color* (see FORMAT-01).

## 7. Formatting and punctuation

**FORMAT-01 — Spelling: international/UK English.** colour, not color. Note the tension with **TERM-01**: *bisque fire*, *wedge*, and *rib* are the craft terms in both varieties, so no conflict arises.

**FORMAT-02 — Product names: sentence case.** "White cunquelo", not "White Cunquelo".

**FORMAT-03 — Quotation marks.** Spanish `"antigua"` → English "old-fashioned" with double curly quotes.

**FORMAT-04 — Serial comma: do not use.** Source *"servir para algo, ser bonito, y evocador"* has one; regularise to English house style without it.

### Pace

The reading should feel unhurried. The text is about a craft that cannot be rushed — *no tengo prisa* — and the punctuation carries that. Sentences are short, warm, plain, vivid.

**Punctuation does not transfer.** A Spanish full stop and an English full stop are not the same event. Spanish prose norms run to longer sentences and tolerate juxtaposition where English requires a full stop or a conjunction. So the Spanish full stop is comparatively rare and heavy; the English one is frequent and light. Copying the source's punctuation mark for mark is not fidelity. It is a category error.

It follows that this source is **marked** in Spanish. Marta's sentences are short *by Spanish standards*, and that shortness is doing the expressive work. Reproduce the same sentence lengths in English and they land at unremarkable English length — the calm is gone, and the text reads as ordinary web copy. To preserve the effect, the English may need to be shorter than the Spanish.

**PACE-01 — Match the effect, not the marks.**
The target is the reader's experience of pace, not a one-to-one mapping of punctuation. Where the two conflict, effect wins. A translator who has produced English of the same average sentence length as the source has probably failed this rule.

**PACE-02 — No em dashes, no semicolons.**
Both accelerate, and neither exists in the source. The em dash is an interruption. The semicolon asks the reader to hold two things at once. Use a full stop.

**PACE-03 — Never join what the source separates.**
A full stop in the source may become a full stop in English. It may not become a comma, an *and*, or a subordinate clause. Sentences may be split. They may not be merged.

**PACE-04 — Plain words.**
Where two words are available, take the shorter, older, more common one. *Becomes*, not *is transformed into*. Latinate diction reads as brochure copy and breaks VOICE-01.

**PACE-05 — Repetition is load-bearing. Preserve it.**
*muchos, muchos siglos.* The doubling is not clumsiness to be tidied. It enacts the duration it describes — the sentence takes longer to say, and so the centuries feel longer. Render it as a doubling: "many, many centuries."

More generally: where the source repeats a word, the translation repeats it. Do not reach for a synonym to avoid an echo. Elegant variation — swapping *pieza* between "piece," "item," and "product" across a paragraph — is a violation, even though it reads as competent English prose. Especially then.

> **Note on testing this section.** These are the softest rules in the guide and the hardest to check. That makes them the most interesting to test.
>
> Note also that PACE-02 is mechanically checkable — count semicolons — while PACE-01 and PACE-03 require reading. And PACE-01, the rule that actually contains the point, is the one no automated check can touch, because it depends on knowing what is normal in each language and noticing that the source departs from it.
>
> A judge that scores terminology at 95% and PACE-01 at chance is telling you something true about what these systems can and cannot see.

## 8. Known traps

Passages where an unassisted translation is expected to fail. Listed so they can be checked deliberately.

**TRAP-01 — The impersonal *se*.**
The whole process description is in impersonal *se*: *se amasa, se centra, se abre, se afina*. English has no equivalent. Two options, and the choice must be consistent across the entire passage:

- **Imperative** — "Wedge the clay, form a ball, place it on the wheel." Reads as instruction to the reader.
- **Passive** — "The clay is wedged, formed into a ball, placed on the wheel." Reads as description.

The source is neither. It is a general, agentless account.

**Decision: use the imperative.** The passive is grammatically the closer match and it is deliberately rejected. It is cold, and it puts the reader outside the studio looking in. The imperative brings them to the wheel. *"Wedge the clay. Form a ball. Place it on the wheel."* The gain in warmth is worth the small shift in who is being addressed.

Consistency across the whole passage is required either way.

**TRAP-02 — *concha marina*.**
*la cunca, que significa concha marina* — the etymological gloss. Translate as "seashell," and keep the gloss structure ("the *cunca*, which means seashell"). Do not drop it as redundant.

**TRAP-03 — *el fondo permanece, pero la superficie cambia*.**
*Fondo* here is "substance / what is underneath," not "background" and not "the bottom of the pot" — though the pun on a vessel's base is live in the Spanish. Suggested: "the substance stays, the surface changes." Any rendering that loses the abstract sense is a violation.

**TRAP-04 — *No tengo una vocación especial por esto*.**
Do not upgrade to "I don't have a particular calling." *Vocación* is doing plain work here. "I have no special vocation for this" is fine; so is "It isn't a calling for me." Not: "I wasn't destined for this."

**TRAP-05 — *loza*.**
*Ten en cuenta que no es loza* — the sentence exists to warn the customer this is **not** earthenware, i.e. it is more fragile than the tableware they own. Translating *loza* as "pottery" or "ceramic" makes the sentence self-contradictory, since the object plainly is both.

## Accuracy

Rules for meaning, added after scoring revealed the guide covered terminology and tone but not fidelity of sense.

**ACC-01 — Do not add adjectives or emphasis absent from the source.**
*las líneas se van afinando* → "the lines become more refined," not "finer and cleaner." One idea in the source is one idea in the target. (Related to VOICE-01; ACC-01 covers descriptive over-translation, not just promo language.)

**ACC-02 — Do not double a word or phrase for emphasis.**
*me encontré un taller frente a la playa* → "I came across a studio facing the beach," not "right across the beach from the beach." No intensifiers ("right," "just") the source doesn't have.

**ACC-03 — Collocations must be idiomatic in English and true to the referent.**
- *el batir de unas piezas contra otras* → the sound of unglazed ceramic is hollow, not metallic. Not "clanging." Use "knocking," "clacking."
- *forma "antigua"... época medieval* → neither "antique" nor "ancient" collocates with medieval pottery form. Use "old" or "old-fashioned."

**ACC-04 — Do not narrow or shift a geographic term.**
*del interior de Galicia* → "inland Galicia" or "the interior of Galicia," not "the Galician countryside." Countryside can be coastal; *interior* specifically means away from the coast. Meaning shift.
